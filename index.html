    Hike and Peep predicts when the leaves will peak across New England, then scores every trail, overlook and scenic drive for each day of the season.

    1. Where you are

      Color comes earlier the higher and farther north you go, and later near the ocean. For every 4 km grid square and every trail, we start from a normal-year peak date based on latitude, elevation (from USGS-derived terrain tiles) and distance to the coast.

    2. What grows there

      Red maples turn first, then sugar maples, birch and beech, and oaks last. Pines and spruce barely change. The USDA Forest Service's map of forest types sets the timing and how vivid each place can get, so a maple ridge scores higher than a spruce flat.

    3. This year's weather

      Cool nights bring color on; a warm September holds it back, and drought makes leaves turn early and dull. Each night we compare this season's temperatures and rainfall (Open-Meteo) with the 2016–2025 average and shift the dates.

    4. What the state forecasts

      In Massachusetts, the Department of Conservation and Recreation publishes its own week-by-week foliage forecast from forestry records and current weather. Near DCR's forecast areas, ours moves halfway toward theirs, and the map shows their date when you tap there.

    5. What people see

      Color reports from the last 12 days pull the forecast toward what's actually on the trees within about 30 km. A single report moves it only part of the way, so one mistaken report can't swing it.

    6. The best day to go

      A trail's score blends how much of its forest is in color (sampled from bottom to top, since a summit can be bare while the trailhead is at peak), how vivid that forest gets, and whether it has a view. For the next two weeks we also count the chance of rain, cloud cover and weekend crowds, with an extra penalty on Indigenous Peoples' Day weekend.

    Grandma mode

    Trails count as easy when they're under about 3 miles round trip with under about 450 ft of climbing, have no rough, rocky or stepped sections, and aren't graded as mountain hiking. Paved or smooth surfaces, benches, restrooms and parking near the start, wheelchair access and loops score higher. Flat out-and-back paths of any length are included, since you can turn around whenever you like. Overlooks count if a road passes within about 100 m or a parking lot is within 150 m.

    Good to know

    • These are predictions. The model is typically within about a week; mountain microclimates, wind storms and early frost can make it worse.
    • Hikes are built from named trail segments in OpenStreetMap. A long trail is shown as one route, so some lengths are a full traverse rather than a typical hike. Distances and climbs are estimates from 30 m terrain data.
    • Surface, bench, restroom and accessibility details depend on volunteer mapping and are often missing. Check before you go, especially in grandma mode.
    • Hike and Peep doesn't use AllTrails data. The AllTrails link on each trail runs a search so you can read recent reviews and conditions there.
